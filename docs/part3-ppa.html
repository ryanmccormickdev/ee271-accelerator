The figure of merit, taken apart

Part 3 grades one number. The handout (section 2.1) defines it, and your goal is to make it small:

FoM = (TINT8 + TINT16 + TINT32)2.3 × Area(µm²) × Power(mW) × 10−15 Tmode is the time the equivalence test for that mode takes, in ns: the cycle count times your clock period. You have to be able to synthesize the design at that period without a timing violation, and the area cap is 1,000,000 µm².

One worked baseline

Take 16 PEs and the synthesis script's default 2.000 ns clock. A design that issues one repetition per cycle (zero bubbles) takes measured 16,440 + 8,248 + 4,152 = 28,840 cycles for the three tests (see ). So T = 28,840 × 2 ns = 57,680 ns, and T2.3 = 8.92 × 1010. That makes

FoM = 8.92 × 10−5 × Area(µm²) × Power(mW)for 16 PEs at 2.000 ns with zero bubbles. illustrative If area were 200,000 µm² and power 30 mW, that is 535.

The absolute number means little on its own. What matters is how it moves when you change something.

Which term is worth the most

Take the log of both sides and the product turns into a sum:

ln FoM = 2.3 · ln T + ln Area + ln Power + constantSo for small changes, ΔFoM/FoM ≈ 2.3·ΔT/T + ΔA/A + ΔP/P. Exactly: FoMnew/FoMold = (Tnew/Told)2.3 · (Anew/Aold) · (Pnew/Pold).

A 10% cut in time is worth about twice a 10% cut in area or in power. Turned around: a change that saves 5% of the time pays for itself until area × power grows by 12.5%. One that saves 10% of the time can afford 27.4%.

What-if

What can actually move the time

T = period × total cycles. For a design that issues one repetition per cycle, the total is fixed by the PE count alone: 1,801 × ceil(256 / P) + 24 cycles on the three official tests (derived and measured on the tab). The compiled program is fixed, and the memories deliver one column per cycle. So the only cycle-count levers are more PEs and not losing cycles to bubbles. Everything else moves T through the clock period.

The FoM adds times, so a cycle saved in INT32 is worth exactly as much as a cycle saved in INT8. INT8 just has more of them: about 57% of the cycles at any PE count, INT16 29%, INT32 14%.

The clock-period trade

Shrinking the period helps T directly, but it moves the other two terms against you:

  • Time scales with the period, and it carries the 2.3 exponent.
  • Dynamic power scales with 1/period. Each cycle costs about the same energy, and there are more cycles per second. Leakage doesn't depend on the clock.
  • Area grows as the target tightens. DC upsizes cells, picks faster (bigger) adder and multiplier structures, and adds buffers. Yosys estimate The same 32×32 multiplier mapped for area is 4,928 µm² (ABC's delay estimate 2.04 ns). Mapped for speed it's 8,563 µm² (1.81 ns): 74% more area for 11% less delay. That's why the last few percent of period are expensive.

If power is proportional to area (more gates, more switching and leakage), these combine into one expression. Here d is the dynamic share of the power at a reference period τ0:

FoM(τ) / FoM(τ0) = (τ/τ0)2.3 × (A(τ)/A0)2 × (d·τ0/τ + 1 − d)With all-dynamic power (d = 1) this is τ1.3 × A²: a 10% shorter period is worth 12.8% if area stays put, and breaks even if area grows 7.1%.

Liberty estimate How big is d? The open Nangate45 Liberty (typical corner) gives cells a leakage of about 15–27 nW per µm² of area: DFF_X1 17.5, NAND2_X1 21.8, INV_X1 27.0. So a 300,000 µm² design leaks roughly 5–8 mW whatever the clock. Your dynamic power comes from the report. The course's .db may be a different corner, so read both numbers off your own power report.

Play with the period trade model

Where this model breaks. DC's area isn't a smooth function of the period. Two runs a few picoseconds apart can differ by several percent, and near the fastest achievable period the area climbs steeply before timing fails. Power isn't exactly proportional to area: flops, which toggle their clock pin every cycle, and logic, which toggles with the data, scale differently. DC's power is itself an estimate built on default switching activity (see ). Use the model to decide what to try, then measure.

The PE-count trade

More PEs mean fewer tiles, so fewer cycles. Area and power grow with the PE count. In the ideal case, with tiles dividing evenly, no fixed cost, and power proportional to area at a fixed clock:

T ∝ 1/P, Area ∝ P, Power ∝ P ⇒ FoM ∝ P−2.3 · P · P = P−0.3Doubling the PE count multiplies the FoM by 2−0.3 = 0.81 (19% better), and you keep winning, slowly, until the area cap.
  • Fixed costs help bigger arrays. The controller, the buffer's address and lane-select logic, and the instruction-memory pointer don't grow with P, so their share shrinks as P grows.
  • Tile rounding hurts most PE counts. The number of tiles is ceil(256 / P) for all three tests, so a PE count is only worth paying for if it's the smallest one that reaches its tile count: P = ceil(256 / t) = 16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 24, 26, 29, 32, 37, 43, 52, 64, 86, 128. Any count in between adds PEs that only compute zero-padding rows. measured 43 and 48 PEs take exactly the same cycles (6,170 / 3,098 / 1,562), so 48 only adds area and power.
  • Per-tile overhead is small. Each tile spends 3 repetitions on CLR, OUT and WRITE, and each test adds 8 cycles. At 128 PEs (2 tiles) the total is 3,626 cycles against an ideal 3,584: 1.2%.

Play with the PE-count trade model

Where this model breaks. The period isn't constant in P. The vector word and the PE instruction are broadcast to every PE, and with the script's set_max_fanout 4 DC builds buffer trees about log4(loads) deep. Those trees sit on the multiplier path, so a big array can need a slower clock. Synthesis run time grows with P too. The defaults above (13,000 µm² per PE, 5,000 fixed) are rough starting values from the tab. Replace them with your own numbers from reports/area_report_hier as soon as you have them.

Quiz yourself

Why is a 10% cut in cycles worth more than a 10% cut in area?

The time sum is raised to the power 2.3 and area to the power 1. In log form, ln FoM = 2.3·ln T + ln A + ln P, so a 10% cut in T multiplies the FoM by 0.92.3 = 0.785 (−21.5%), while a 10% cut in area gives 0.9 (−10%).

You shorten the clock period by 10% and nothing else changes. What happens to each term?

T drops 10% (FoM × 0.785). Dynamic power rises by 1/0.9 = 11% (leakage doesn't change). Area stays the same only in this thought experiment; in reality DC grows it. With all-dynamic power the net is 0.91.3 = 0.872, a 12.8% improvement, and it breaks even if area grows 7.1%.

Why is 43 PEs better than 48 PEs for this FoM?

Both need ceil(256/P) = 6 tiles, so they take exactly the same cycles (measured 6,170 / 3,098 / 1,562). 48 PEs just add 5 PEs of area and power that compute zero-padding rows.

A cycle saved in INT32 or a cycle saved in INT8: which is worth more?

The same. The FoM adds the three times before raising to 2.3, so every cycle saved anywhere shortens T by one clock period.

Why can't any optimization except more PEs (or fixing bubbles) reduce the cycle count?

The program comes from the staff compiler and runs cols + 3 repetitions per tile. The memories deliver one matrix column per cycle, so one repetition per cycle is the ceiling. Fewer tiles needs more PEs. Overlapping CLR/OUT/WRITE could save at most 3 cycles per tile, about 1% of the FoM.

The cycle model

Heads-upEvery count on this tab was measured with a throwaway zero-bubble reference design that lives only in a scratch folder, never in the repo. It issues one repetition per cycle, and each PE operation happens one cycle after its memory operation. That is one valid timing choice, not the only one, and this tab shows its consequences. If you want to work the Part 2 timing out yourself first, do the controller and buffer before reading on. Your own design may differ by a constant (fine) or by bubbles (worth fixing; the bottom of this tab says how much).

Step 1: what the program does per tile

The staff compiler (part1/src/compiler.py:88-91, the same file as part2/simulator/src/compiler.py) emits four instructions per tile of rows:

Instruction (as text)Memory opPE opRepetitions
NOP | CLR INTx | 1 0 0nothingclear acc and out1
READ INTx base 0 | MAC INTx | cols 1 1read one column of the tile and one vector elementmultiply-accumulatecols
NOP | OUT INTx | 1 0 0nothingclamp acc into out1
WRITE tile | NOP INTx | 1 0 0store all PE outputs as one MEM2 wordnothing1

The count you write is the number of repetitions; the assembler stores count − 1. So one tile is cols + 3 repetitions.

Step 2: how many tiles

Rows per tile = P × lanes (compiler.py:68, lanes = 32 / mode width: 4, 2, 1). Tiles = ceil(rows / (P × lanes)) (compiler.py:69). The three official tests (part2/equivalence_testing/Makefile) are INT8 1024×1024, INT16 512×512 and INT32 256×256. That gives 1024/(4P), 512/(2P) and 256/P: every test has ceil(256 / P) tiles.

Step 3: what the hardware and the testbench add

  • One extra instruction. The testbench is told INST_COUNT = lines in mat_mul.asm + 1 (equivalence Makefile, line 51). ivd-as pads the instruction file with all-zero words, and the extra word decodes as READ INT8 0 0 | MAC INT8 with count 0: one harmless repetition.
  • Reset. rst_n is low for 5 clock periods (tb_top.sv:5-6, 100-105). The clock's first rising edge after reset is at 55 ns.
  • The PE stage. In this design pe_inst_valid is a one-cycle-delayed copy of buf_inst_valid, so the pipeline drains one cycle after the last memory op.
  • The end check. simulation_complete = (pc == instruction_count) && ~pe_inst_valid && ~buf_inst_valid (tb_top.sv:129). On its rising edge the testbench waits one more CLK_PERIOD, then dumps MEM2 and prints $time / 10.

A toy run: 2 tiles of a 3-column matrix (R = 2 × 6 + 1 = 13 repetitions)

Each box is one 10 ns clock cycle. Memory ops issue in a cycle, their PE ops run one cycle later, and a WRITE commits at the end of the cycle after it issues.

With R repetitions, the last PE op ends at 65 + 10R ns, the dump happens at 75 + 10R ns, and integer division gives (75 + 10R) / 10 = R + 7. With R = tiles × (cols + 3) + 1:

cycles = tiles × (cols + 3) + 8, tiles = ceil(rows / (P × lanes))The 8 is 5 reset cycles, 1 for the all-zero word, 1 for the PE stage to drain, and 1 for the testbench's wait. The first edge comes half a cycle after reset ends, and the integer division drops that half again.

Summed over the three official tests: Σ cycles = 1,801 × ceil(256 / P) + 24, because 1027 + 515 + 259 = 1,801.

The instruction-memory depth trap

A program has 4 × tiles instructions. Three things must fit:

  • ivd-as writes exactly --controller_imem_depth words (default 128): the program, then zero words. A longer program is cut off without a warning (ivd-as:41-48).
  • The program plus its all-zero word must fit in the depth.
  • INST_COUNT = lines + 1 is loaded into instruction_count, which is only IMEM_ADDR_WIDTH = ceil(log2 depth) bits wide (ivd-gv). A bigger number silently wraps.

For the official tests that means 4 × ceil(256 / P) + 2 ≤ depth. The default depth of 128 works for P ≥ 9. P = 5 to 8 need 256, and P = 4 needs 512. If you go below 9 PEs, pass the same --controller_imem_depth to ivd-gv, ivd-as and ivd-sim. measured Here is what happens if you don't (Icarus, INT8, default depth):

Two more sizing traps. (1) part2/unoptimized_verilog/synth/Makefile runs ivd-gv --out defines.sv with no --pe_count, so synthesis builds 16 PEs no matter what your tests used. Change both. (2) The staff reference simulator can't run the INT8 test with 4 PEs. It bumps MemAOffset after the last repetition too, and 63 × 1024 + 1024 = 65,536 doesn't fit the 16-bit field (ValueError from accelerator.py:113). The INT8 test needs P ≥ 5.

MEM0 and MEM2 width

MEM0 and MEM2 are P × 32 bits wide (compiler.py:26, 30): 512 bits at 16 PEs, 4,096 bits at 128. Their depth is 216 = 65,536 entries, and a test uses tiles × cols MEM0 entries (65,536 for INT8 at P = 4, which is why that case sits right on the edge). In synthesis the memories are empty black boxes (synth/macros/array.sv), so their width costs no area in the FoM. In a real chip a 4,096-bit-wide SRAM port would be its own design problem.

What bubbles cost

measured Two deliberately slowed copies of the reference controller (scratch only), run through the same three official tests (Icarus):

One idle cycle per instruction costs 4 cycles per tile: tiles × (cols + 7) + 8, about +1.5% FoM at 16 PEs. Taking two cycles per repetition (read in one cycle, compute in the next, no overlap) doubles everything: 2 × tiles × (cols + 3) + 8, about 4.9 times the FoM. By contrast, an extra pipeline stage that doesn't stall adds about one cycle per test at the end, +3 cycles of 28,840: +0.02% FoM.

One cycle of difference: in the one-bubble variant at 16 PEs, the testbench sometimes reports one cycle less. pe_inst_valid falls and buf_inst_valid rises at the same clock edge while pc == instruction_count, and always @(posedge simulation_complete) fires on that zero-width glitch. A pipeline that lets both valids drop for even a moment before its last WRITE commits gets dumped early.

Quiz yourself

Derive the INT8 cycle count for 16 PEs.

Rows per tile = 16 × 4 = 64, so tiles = 1024 / 64 = 16. Each tile is 1024 + 3 = 1,027 repetitions, so 16,432, plus 1 for the all-zero word, gives R = 16,433. The testbench adds 5 reset cycles, 1 for the PE stage and 1 for its wait: 16,440.

Why do all three official tests have the same number of tiles?

1024 rows / (P × 4 lanes) = 512 / (P × 2) = 256 / (P × 1) = 256 / P. The tests were sized so that each mode's lanes exactly offset its row count.

Your design with 8 PEs fails int8_test but passes int16 and int32. What do you check first?

The instruction memory depth. 8 PEs give 32 tiles = 128 instructions. With the default depth of 128 there's no room for the zero word, and INST_COUNT = 129 wraps to 1 in the 7-bit instruction_count. Pass --controller_imem_depth 256 to ivd-gv, ivd-as and ivd-sim. (int16/int32 have the same tile count, so they'd fail too if they used 8 PEs. If they pass, check that every Makefile target really got the same --pe_count.)

What does the "+8" consist of?

5 reset cycles, 1 repetition for the all-zero padding instruction, 1 cycle for the PE operation that trails the last memory operation, and 1 cycle the testbench waits after simulation_complete. The half-cycle offset of the first clock edge is cancelled by the integer division.

Your pipelined design reports 3 more cycles per test than before. Is that a problem?

No, if it's a constant: 9 more cycles out of 28,840 is +0.07% FoM. It is a problem if the count grows with the number of tiles or instructions (that's a bubble), or if MEM2 is dumped before the last WRITE (a valid signal dropped too early).

Hardware reuse: four multipliers for every mode

  
The handout (2.2.1) says the usual PE uses 4 8-bit, 2 16-bit and 1 32-bit multipliers, that only one mode is used at a time, and that "it is possible to use a total of 4 or less multipliers." This tab is the arithmetic behind that sentence. It is the math, not the RTL.

  
Heads-upThe demo below computes exactly what a shared multiplier array has to compute, in JavaScript. Treat it like the walkthrough's model.js: it's effectively the reference arithmetic. Try to derive the split yourself first, then use the demo to check your edge cases.


  
Why seven multipliers is wasteful

  
Every PE runs one mode at a time. A PE written as "one * per lane per mode" can end up with seven multipliers that all compute every cycle on the same input wires, while a mode mux throws six results away. Yosys estimate Those seven are about 9,023 µm² per PE (4 × 361 + 2 × 1,324 + 4,928, Booth-encoded, mapped for area). Four 17×17 multipliers are about 6,357 µm². DC may already share some operators across your case branches, so before you change anything, look at what it built: report_resources lists every multiplier and adder it inferred and which DesignWare implementation it chose. Treat it as a commonly used command and check it exists on caddy's DC.


  
The math: one 32×32 product from four 17×17 products

  
Split each 32-bit signed operand into halves. The high half keeps the sign; the low half is just bits, an unsigned number from 0 to 65,535:

  
a = aH · 216 + aL,  aH = signed a[31:16],  aL = unsigned a[15:0]Check with a = −5 = 0xFFFFFFFB: aH = 0xFFFF = −1 and aL = 0xFFFB = 65,531, and −1 · 65,536 + 65,531 = −5.

  
Multiply out:

  
a · b = aHbH · 232 + (aHbL + aLbH) · 216 + aLbL

  
    
    
  • Why 17 bits. Each partial product mixes signed and unsigned halves. A signed multiplier handles both if every operand gets a 17th bit on top: a copy of the sign bit for a signed half, a 0 for an unsigned half. So 65,531 becomes the positive 17-bit number 0_1111111111111011, and −1 becomes 1_1111111111111111.
    • 
    
  • How wide each product is. aHbH fits in 32 signed bits (its extremes are about ±230). Each cross term aHbL or aLbH just fits in 32 signed bits too (−32,768 × 65,535 = −2,147,450,880), but their sum needs 33. aLbL is at most 65,535² < 232, an unsigned 32-bit number.
    • 
    
  • A free concatenation. Because aLbL < 232, the outer terms don't overlap: aHbH · 232 + aLbL is just {aHbH[31:0], aLbL[31:0]}. Only the two cross terms, sign-extended and shifted left 16, need a real addition.
    • 
    
  • The accumulate. In INT32 mode the 64-bit accumulator gets acc + concatenation + cross term: a three-input add, which a carry-save stage (a row of full adders) reduces to one ordinary adder.
    • 
  

  
INT16 uses two of the same four multipliers. Lane 1 is a[31:16] × b[31:16], which is exactly aHbH. Lane 0 is a[15:0] × b[15:0], but now both halves are signed, so the low operands get their sign bit as the 17th bit instead of a 0. The two cross multipliers sit idle; hold their inputs at 0 so they don't toggle. The same concatenation {lane 1, lane 0} drops each lane product into its 32-bit accumulator lane. The carry between bit 31 and bit 32 must be killed.

  
INT8 uses all four, one 8×8 lane each, with 8-bit operands sign-extended to 17 bits. The products are 16 bits, concatenated into the four 16-bit accumulator lanes. Carries are killed at bits 16, 32 and 48. Which multiplier takes which lane is your choice; pick whatever makes the input muxes simplest.

  
So each multiplier input is a small mux steered by the PE instruction's mode field. Its 17th bit is either a sign copy or 0, depending on the mode. That one bit is where most sharing bugs live.

Applying it to this design

  

    
In this design
Only the multiply-add part of MAC changes. CLR, OUT, PASS and RND, the accumulator lanes, and the timing of everything around the PE stay as they are. The mode that steers the input muxes is the PE instruction's mode (pe_inst.mode), the same field that already picks the lane layout.

    
Expected effect
Yosys estimate Multiplier area per PE goes from about 9,000 to 6,400 µm². Input muxes and the extra carry-save stage take back a few hundred, so expect a net of about −2,000 µm² per PE, roughly 15% of a naive PE. If power falls with area, the FoM improves about 1 − 0.85² ≈ 28%. Cycles don't change. The period may move either way: muxes in front, a 17×17 instead of a 32×32 inside (ABC estimates 1.35 ns against 2.04 ns), and a three-input add behind. The freed area can also buy more PEs.

    
Risks
  • The 17th bit per mode: zero-extension for the low halves in INT32, sign-extension in INT16 and INT8.
  • Carries leaking across lane boundaries, in every adder in the chain, including the carry vector of a carry-save stage.
  • A cross-term sum that's one bit too narrow (it needs 33 bits before the shift).
  • The official tests use values −5…4, so products are tiny and many high-bit bugs never show. Negative values do exercise the halves (aH = −1). Test the PE with random full-range operands in all three modes against $signed(a) * $signed(b), and run make eq-stress.

    
How to measure
Per-PE area in reports/area_report_hier before and after; report_resources to confirm four multipliers; the timing report, to see whether the worst path still goes through a multiplier and how long it is; the power report; then the FoM. The cycle counts should be identical.

    
What to write
"We replaced the per-mode multipliers with four 17×17 signed multipliers whose operands are selected by mode (sign- or zero-extended 16-bit halves in INT32, sign-extended lanes in INT16/INT8). PE area went from X to Y µm² (−Z%), power from … to …, and the FoM improved by W%, almost all of it from the area and power terms because cycles and period were unchanged." Add one sentence on what the timing report showed.

  

  
A different route. One 32×32 array can also produce two 16×16 or four 8×8 products if you zero the partial-product blocks that would mix lanes (a "partitioned" or subword-parallel multiplier). That is fewer gates than four separate multipliers. But you'd build the partial-product array yourself instead of writing *, which gives up DesignWare's tuned multiplier. Quiz yourself

    
How do four multipliers compute a 32×32 product?
Split a and b into a signed high half and an unsigned low half. Compute aHbH, aHbL, aLbH, aLbL on 17×17 signed multipliers (17th bit = sign copy for a signed half, 0 for an unsigned half). Then a·b = aHbH·232 + (aHbL + aLbH)·216 + aLbL, where the outer two terms are a plain concatenation.

    
Why 17×17 and not 16×16?
An unsigned 16-bit half (0 to 65,535) doesn't fit a 16-bit signed input. Adding a 17th bit lets one signed multiplier handle signed×signed, signed×unsigned and unsigned×unsigned. The alternative is 16×16 unsigned multipliers plus correction terms for the signs.

    
In INT16 mode, what's different about the low operands compared with INT32?
Their 17th bit. In INT32 the low half is unsigned, so the 17th bit is 0. In INT16 the low half is lane 0, a signed number, so the 17th bit copies bit 15.

    
Why might the official tests not catch a sharing bug?
Their values are −5…4, so products are tiny and bits far above the bottom never carry information. Bugs that only matter for large magnitudes (a cross-term sum truncated to 32 bits, a carry leaking into a neighboring lane at bit 48) can pass. Randomized full-range PE tests and eq-stress catch those.

Register retiming and manual pipelining

    

      
What retiming is

        
Retiming moves existing registers across combinational logic so the delays on either side even out, without changing what the circuit does at its inputs and outputs. DC does it when you pass -retime to compile_ultra and set set_optimize_registers true. The staff script already does both (dc_synth.tcl:68-69), so your baseline is already retimed.

        
What it can't do:

        
  • Add registers. It only moves the ones you wrote, so to get more stages you add registers in RTL and let retiming place them. That's what the handout means by "instantiate a series of registers and allow the synthesis tool to optimize them."
  • Change latency. The number of registers on every input-to-output path stays the same.
  • Shorten a loop. A feedback loop with one register keeps all its logic inside one cycle, wherever the register sits.
  • Move a register across a black box. The SRAM outputs here are black-box pins, so there's no register on the memory side for it to pull into the multiplier.

      
In this design

        
The PE's long path starts at a register that steers the multiplier's operands, for example one that selects the vector lane or holds the PE instruction. The path runs through the multiplier and the 64-bit add and ends at the accumulator. To cut it, register something in the middle: the product (or the four partial products). Then stage 1 multiplies and stage 2 accumulates, and -retime can slide that register into the multiplier tree to balance the stages.

        
The accumulator update itself is a loop, acc ← acc + product, and it must close every cycle: one more MAC arrives every cycle. Pipelining the multiplier leaves the 64-bit add plus the next-state mux as the loop. To cut that, you need a different representation, such as carry-save accumulation: keep acc as two vectors and add each product with a row of full adders (constant delay, whatever the width), resolving them with a real adder only when OUT or RND needs the value. It costs a second 64-bit register per PE and an adder on the OUT/RND path. RND (shift and round, acc → acc) is a loop too. Once the multiplier is pipelined, it may become the worst path even though the tests never run it, because DC times every path.

      
System-level consequences (the hard part)

        
  • OUT after the last MAC. Every PE op must see the accumulator after the previous op's update. If MAC writes acc in stage 2, OUT has to read acc in stage 2 (or later), or it reads it one MAC too early.
  • WRITE after OUT. The program puts WRITE right after OUT with no spacer. With a deeper PE, the OUT result reaches the output register later, so the WRITE has to commit later by the same number of cycles. For example, delay the buffer's write strobe and address, or delay the buffer op in the controller.
  • The testbench's end check. It dumps MEM2 one cycle after pc reaches instruction_count while dut.pe_inst_valid and dut.buf_inst_valid are both low. Keep a valid high until the last WRITE has committed, and never let both drop at the same edge, even for an instant: the testbench fires on a zero-width glitch (measured, see the  tab).
  • No stalls. Handle the hazards with matched delays, not bubbles. One bubble per instruction costs about 1.5% FoM; stalling every MAC would double the cycles.

      
Expected effect

        
Cycles: about +1 per test per added stage at the end (+0.02% FoM each). Area: one 64-bit product register is about 290–340 µm² per PE Yosys estimate, more if retiming duplicates flops. Power: more clocked flops, and dynamic power rises as the period falls. Period: the big lever, if the multiplier dominates the worst path today.

        
illustrative If one stage took the period from 2.5 to 1.5 ns and area grew 5%: with power ∝ area/period, FoM × (1.5/2.5)1.3 × 1.052 = 0.51 × 1.10 = 0.57, about 43% better. That's a guess at the shape, not a prediction; measure it.

      
How to measure

        
Compare the timing report before and after: the worst path's startpoint, endpoint and arrival time should change, ending at your new register or starting from it. Look in compile.log for what the retiming step reports. Check the cycle counts (a constant +1 or +2) and run the official tests and make eq-stress on Icarus. Look at the waveform around one OUT → WRITE pair.

      
What to write

        
"We registered the product, so a MAC multiplies in one stage and accumulates in the next, and delayed the WRITE commit by one more cycle so it captures the OUT result. With -retime DC moved the register into the multiplier tree. The worst path went from [startpoint → endpoint, X ns] to [..., Y ns], so we lowered the clock from A to B ns. Cycles grew by 1 per test. The FoM improved by Z%: the time term contributed …, area and power …."

    

    
Carry-save, in one picture: a full adder takes three bits and returns a sum bit and a carry bit (the carry belongs one position to the left). A row of 64 of them turns three numbers into two in one full-adder delay, because no carry ripples across the row. A sum kept as two numbers (S, C) can absorb a new product every cycle that way. Only reading the value needs a real adder: S + C.

Ways to cut a path, roughly in order of cost: register in the middle (pipelining, as above). Precompute: decode the mode into one-hot select signals a cycle early and register them. Restructure, for example by moving a mux from in front of the multiplier to behind it. Clock gating

    

      
What it is, and what DC does

        
A register that only sometimes loads is, in plain logic, a flop with a 2:1 mux in front: D = enable ? new : Q. It still burns clock power every cycle. Clock gating replaces the hold mux with an integrated clock-gating cell (ICG) that stops the clock to the whole register bank when the enable is 0. The Nangate library has CLKGATETST_X1 (3.99 µm², a latch plus an AND gate with a test-enable pin) and CLKGATE_X1 (3.458 µm²). DC inserts them where it finds load-enable registers.

        
Yosys estimate A 64-bit register with a load enable and no gating is 408.6 µm² (64 DFF_X1 + 64 MUX2_X1). Without the muxes it's 289.4 µm², plus one 3.99 µm² ICG: about −115 µm² per 64-bit register, before counting the clock power saved.

      
The RTL style that exposes enables

        
Write the enable in the clocked block, so DC infers a load-enable register:


always_ff @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n)
  if (!rst_n)      out_q <= '0;
  else if (out_en) out_q <= out_next;   // no else: holds

        
A hold written as a default in always_comb (next = q unless …) and registered every cycle may not be recognized as an enable. report_clock_gating tells you which registers were gated and which weren't.

      
Which registers idle in this design

        
From the program structure (per official test, per tile of cols + 3 repetitions):

        
  • PE output register (32 bits × P): loads only on OUT and CLR, 2 of every cols + 3 repetitions. That's busy 0.19% of the cycles in INT8, 0.39% in INT16, 0.77% in INT32. The best candidate.
  • Controller instruction register: loads once per instruction, 4 times per tile.
  • Buffer write address, if it's registered every cycle: only WRITE needs it.
  • PE accumulator (64 bits × P): loads on MAC and CLR, (cols + 1)/(cols + 3) of the cycles, 99.8% in INT8. Gating it saves almost nothing in the real test.
  • Pipeline registers you add: busy whenever MACs flow, about 99%.

      
How DC's default activity skews the savings

        
Without simulation data, report_power estimates switching activity on its own (vectorless). Primary inputs and black-box outputs get default toggle rates and static probabilities, and DC propagates them through the logic. Here almost everything starts at black-box SRAM outputs. A gating enable is a decode of instruction bits, like "opcode is OUT or CLR", so the probability DC assigns it comes from that propagation. It has no knowledge that 99.7% of the PE instructions in the INT8 test are MACs. So the reported saving can be far from what the chip would see running the test. Gating the accumulator might show a large "saving" in the report although in the real test it loads almost every cycle.

        
The FoM uses DC's reported power, so both numbers matter. In the report, say which activity assumption the power number rests on, and explain the change from the real duty cycles above.

      
Commands (commonly used; check them on caddy)

        
  • set_clock_gating_style (for example -sequential_cell latch -positive_edge_logic {integrated} -minimum_bitwidth 4) and then compile_ultra -gate_clock. Older flows use insert_clock_gating.
  • report_clock_gating: how many registers were gated and why the others weren't.
  • report_power: compare the register and clock_network rows before and after.
  • report_switching_activity: what activity DC assumed.

        
Option names change between DC versions, so read man compile_ultra on the version caddy runs before you trust any of these. Ask on Ed whether you may edit dc_synth.tcl for this. The handout leaves "the exact steps … as an exercise for the reader", which suggests yes.

      
Expected effect, and SAIF

        
Area: small, the hold muxes minus one ICG per bank (about −55 µm² per PE for the 32-bit output register). Power: real-activity savings come mostly from the output register and controller registers. Their size depends on the "register" share of your power report, roughly register share × (gated bits / all bits) × (1 − duty cycle). Cycles and period: unchanged, if the enable's timing fits (the ICG latch needs the enable before the clock edge, like a flop's D input).

        
"Anything else" idea: SAIF. Record real switching activity from simulation: VCS can write a SAIF file, or Synopsys vcd2saif converts a VCD. Design sizing: the PE count and the clock period

    

      
PE-count sweep

        
  1. Only try tile-efficient counts: P = ceil(256 / tiles) = 16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 24, 26, 29, 32, 37, 43, 52, 64, 86, 128. Anything else adds PEs without removing a tile.
  2. Synthesize at 2 or 3 points (say 16, 32, 64) at one period. Fit area ≈ Afixed + P · APE (and the same for power). From that, predict the area cap and the FoM curve (the ), then confirm the best point with a real run.
  3. Recheck the period at the chosen P: bigger arrays mean deeper broadcast buffer trees.
  4. Change --pe_count everywhere: the three tools in the equivalence Makefile (ivd-gv, ivd-cc, ivd-sim; the handout's 2.2.5 lists these), ivd-as if you go below 9 PEs (imem depth), and the synth Makefile's ivd-gv.

      
Clock-period sweep (binary search)

        
  1. Find a period that passes (hi) and one that fails (lo).
  2. Synthesize at mid = (lo + hi)/2 with make synthesize CLK_PERIOD=mid. If the worst slack is ≥ 0, set hi = mid, else lo = mid.
  3. Stop when hi − lo ≤ 0.05 ns, or when the runs get too slow.

        
Run make clean between runs: dc_synth.tcl appends to results.txt (>>), so without it the file collects several runs. The calculator warns if it sees more than one.

      
Things to know

        
  • DC isn't monotonic: a tighter target can pass where a looser one failed, and area grows as you tighten. Compute the FoM at every passing point and keep the best. It's often a little above the fastest passing period.
  • Use the period you synthesized at, with that run's area and power. "2.0 ns with +0.3 ns of slack" does not let you claim 1.7 ns; you'd have to synthesize at 1.7 and pass.
  • The sweep is also the first thing to redo after every other optimization: the best P and period move.
  • Big P means long DC runs. Time your first run and plan around it. Separate synth folders let runs go in parallel, because outputs go to fixed names in the current folder.

      
Expected effect

        
From the model: each doubling of P is worth about −19% while tiles divide evenly and area stays under the cap. The period sweep is worth whatever slack your baseline leaves on the table. Both are cheap in effort and need no RTL change, which is why they come first.

Quiz yourself

    
The script already uses -retime. Why didn't it fix your timing?
Retiming only moves registers you already have, and it can't shorten a loop. The long path runs from a register through the multiplier and adder into the accumulator, a one-register loop with nothing in the middle to move. You add a pipeline register (for example on the product), and retiming then places it well.

    
Why must the accumulate step close in one cycle?
Each MAC adds to the result of the previous one, and a new MAC arrives every cycle. If acc + product took two cycles, the next MAC would read an accumulator that doesn't include its predecessor yet. Ways around it change the representation (carry-save) or the schedule (stalls, which cost cycles).

    
You add one pipeline stage to the PE. What else in the system must change?
OUT must read the accumulator after the last MAC's update, so do it in the same or a later stage. The WRITE that follows OUT has to commit one cycle later than before. A valid signal the testbench watches has to stay high until that last WRITE commits, without a glitch where both drop.

    
Why can clock-gating the accumulator change DC's reported power a lot but the real power hardly at all?
In the real test the accumulator loads in about 99.8% of INT8 cycles (MAC), so gating rarely stops its clock. DC's vectorless analysis doesn't know the program. It propagates default probabilities to the enable, which can make the register look idle much of the time, so the report credits a saving the workload wouldn't see.

    
Your design passes at 1.8 ns and fails at 1.7 ns. What period do you use in the FoM?
1.8 ns, with the area and power from the 1.8 ns run. If you think something between them passes, synthesize there; positive slack at 1.8 doesn't entitle you to a shorter period. Also compare the FoM at 1.8 against slightly looser periods, because area and power change too.

What they add up to rough estimate

  

  
The rest of a naive PE (adders for each lane layout, the next-state muxes for acc and out, clamping, and the RND shifter and rounding) is a few thousand µm² more, depending on how you write it. So a naive PE lands somewhere around 12,000–15,000 µm² in this estimate, and the multipliers are most of it.

  
Ballpark PE count under the cap: at 13,000 µm² per PE plus a few thousand fixed, about 1,000,000 / 13,000 ≈ 75 PEs fit. The nearest tile-efficient sizes are 64 (4 tiles) and 86 (3 tiles); 86 needs a PE under about 11,500 µm², and 128 needs under about 7,700 µm². DC at a tight clock will likely be bigger than Yosys's area-oriented mapping, which pushes these down. Treat it as "64 probably fits; anything bigger is a question your own area report answers."

Quiz yourself

    
Why are four 17×17 multipliers bigger than one 32×32, yet sharing still saves area?
One 32×32 Booth multiplier shares its whole reduction tree. Four separate ones each have their own tree and final adder, so 4 × 1,589 = 6,357 µm² against 4,928. But the naive PE has seven multipliers (about 9,023 µm²), and the four shared ones replace all seven.

    
Why can't you use these numbers in your report?
They're from Yosys, not Design Compiler. DC uses DesignWare components and sizes cells for your clock and constraints. The FoM must come from your own synthesis runs. These are only for planning.

    
A register with a load enable costs 408.6 µm² for 64 bits, one without costs 289.4. Where does the difference go with clock gating?
The 64 hold muxes (MUX2_X1, 1.862 µm² each) disappear. One clock-gating cell (CLKGATETST_X1, 3.99 µm²) stops the clock for the whole bank instead.

A real buffer bug that drops the last MAC operand passes all three. eq-stress uses other seeds and sizes that aren't tile multiples, and optimizations like pipelining and sharing are where such bugs show up.

    
Run A improves the FoM 20% and run B, on top of A, another 20%. What's the total improvement?
36%: the ratios multiply, 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64. Percentages don't add.

    
Why do the period and PE sweeps come first?
They need no RTL change, they're cheap to run, and their effect is large (period through the 2.3 exponent, P through the tile count). They also set the configuration every later experiment is measured against.

  











  
Words this page uses

  

    
FoM (figure of merit)
The single number Part 3 grades: (sum of the three test times)2.3 × area × power × 10−15. Lower is better.

    
PPA
Power, performance, area: the three things the FoM combines.

    
Tile
The group of matrix rows the PE array works on at once: P × lanes rows. The compiler emits 4 instructions per tile.

    
Lane
One independent number inside a 32-bit word: 4 lanes in INT8, 2 in INT16, 1 in INT32.

    
Repetition
One pass of an instruction. An instruction runs count + 1 times, its stored count plus one.

    
Bubble
A cycle in which no repetition is issued. Zero-bubble means one repetition every cycle, including across instruction boundaries.

    
Clock period, frequency
Time between rising clock edges (ns), and its inverse. Dynamic power grows with frequency.

    
Critical path
The slowest register-to-register path. It sets the shortest period that meets timing.

    
Arrival, required, setup
When data gets to a flop's input; the latest it may get there (period − setup time); and how long before the clock edge a flop needs its input stable.

    
Slack, WNS, TNS
Slack = required − arrival; negative means a violation. WNS is the worst slack, TNS the sum of all negative slacks.

    
Retiming
Moving existing registers across logic to balance stage delays without changing behavior at the boundaries.

    
Pipelining
Adding registers to split long logic into stages. Throughput can stay one per cycle while latency grows.

    
Latency, throughput
Cycles from an operation's start to its result, and operations finished per cycle.

    
Hazard
An operation reading a value before an earlier operation has written it, here OUT before the last MAC, or WRITE before OUT.

    
Carry-save
Keeping a sum as two numbers (sum bits and carry bits) so a new term can be added with a row of full adders in constant time. A real adder resolves it when needed.

    
CPA, carry kill
A carry-propagate adder is an ordinary adder. A carry kill forces the carry between two lanes to 0 so the lanes stay independent.

    
Booth encoding, reduction tree
The usual multiplier structure: fewer partial products (Booth), summed by a tree of full adders, then one final adder. DesignWare picks the variant.

    
DesignWare
Synopsys's library of arithmetic building blocks (dw_foundation.sldb in the script). DC implements * and + with them.

    
Partial product
One piece of a product, such as aHbL, summed after shifting to make the whole product.

    
Sign and zero extension
Widening a number by copying its sign bit (keeps a signed value) or by adding zeros (keeps an unsigned value).

    
Clock gating, ICG
Stopping the clock of registers that won't load. An integrated clock-gating cell (latch + AND) does it glitch-free. Nangate: CLKGATETST_X1.

    
Load enable
A register input that says "load this cycle"; without gating it's a hold mux in front of every flop.

    
Dynamic, internal, switching, leakage power
Dynamic = internal (inside cells as they switch) + switching (charging wires and inputs). Leakage flows even when nothing switches.

    
Switching activity, toggle rate, static probability
How often a net changes per cycle, and how much of the time it's 1. DC needs them to estimate dynamic power.

    
Vectorless estimate, SAIF
Power estimated from default, propagated activity, versus activity annotated from a simulation, stored in a SAIF (Switching Activity Interchange Format) file.

    
Operand isolation
Holding the inputs of unused logic constant so it doesn't toggle.

    
Black box
A module with ports and no contents. Here the SRAMs in synthesis: no area, no timing through them.

    
Fanout, buffer tree
How many inputs a signal drives. The script caps it at 4, so DC inserts trees of buffers for signals broadcast to every PE.

    
Wire load model
DC's guess of wire capacitance from fanout, before any layout (here 1K_hvratio_1_4).

    
Upsizing
Replacing a cell with a stronger, bigger version (X1 → X2 → X4) to make a path faster.

    
Log-sensitivity
How much ln FoM moves per unit change in ln of an input: 2.3 for time, 1 for area, 1 for power.

    
Tile-efficient P
A PE count with no wasted rows on the last tile for these tests: P = ceil(256 / tiles).

    
Glitch
A zero-width or very short pulse on a combinational signal, for example when two inputs change at the same edge.

    
Duty cycle
The fraction of cycles in which a signal, here a register's load enable, is active.

    
Yosys, ABC
Open-source synthesis tools used for this page's estimates. Yosys reads the Verilog and calls ABC to map the logic onto library cells. They are not Design Compiler.

    
Parallel-prefix (Kogge-Stone) adder
An adder that computes all carries in log2(width) levels instead of letting them ripple. It's faster than a ripple-carry adder and bigger.
